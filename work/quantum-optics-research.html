Abstract

In spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC), one pump photon splits into a signal and an idler photon inside a nonlinear crystal. This only happens efficiently when energy and momentum both balance, a condition called phase matching. In a birefringent crystal such as lithium niobate (LiNbO3), that balance depends on wavelength, polarization and the propagation angle relative to the optic axis. I built Python models that map ray separation and internal walk-off and solve for the discrete phase-matching conditions. One of them is an interactive tool that locks any one wavelength and returns the experimental geometry for the rest.

1The phase-matching problem

Finding the discrete set of conditions that produce entangled photon pairs is one of the main hurdles in setting up an SPDC experiment. Two relations must hold at once:

1λp=1λs+1λi (1) energy
Δk=kp−ks−ki=0 (2a) momentum
npλp=nsλs+niλi (2b) collinear

Normal dispersion makes (2b) impossible with a single polarization. A birefringent crystal fixes this: polarize the pump as the extraordinary wave, and its index depends on the angle θ to the optic axis.

1ne2(θ)=cos2θno2+sin2θne2 (3) index ellipsoid

2Ordinary and extraordinary rays

Ordinary and extraordinary waves see different indices along different axes, so they separate inside the crystal. I started with the simplest case: a 2D map of that angular separation for LiNbO3 across wavelength and incident angle, built from published equations and experimental data. Everything else rests on it.

3Internal path deflection

As the incident angle and the angle from the optic axis change, the beam’s internal path deviates from the expected direction. Refraction and spatial walk-off combine. I mapped the full evolution of that deflection and used it to guide phase matching and experimental setup parameters.

tan⁡ρ=ne2(θ)2(1ne2−1no2)sin⁡2θ (4) walk-off

4The phase-matching model

Heatmaps only show part of the picture. I wrote a Python program, based on several graduate-level analyses of discrete solutions, that solves for the phase-matching conditions directly. On top of it sits an interactive heatmap: lock the pump, signal or idler wavelength, and it shows the crystal angle that produces entangled photons and the wavelengths they will appear at. It is built for LiNbO3’s material properties, and the material parameters can be swapped for other crystals.

For Type II the solution reduces to a quartic in tan θ (Fig. 03.7), which the model solves for each wavelength set.

Fig. 03.6 — Recording

The model in use: locking each wavelength in turn

The pump is locked at 1.055 µm while the signal is dragged. Then the signal is locked and the pump swept, then the idler, and finally a pump at 805.6 nm is swept through degeneracy. The ring is where phase-matching efficiency (sinc²) peaks.

5In the lab

Optical measurements at a doctoral level take quantum and optical theory, plus the electrical engineering and signal analysis to execute them properly. I don’t claim mastery of any of it, but I rose to it. I worked with interferometers, assisted with second-harmonic generation setups, did precision optical alignment, and worked with electromagnetic fields, currents and cooling chambers. The measurements I took supported the doctoral students’ work. In simulation, I used Ansys Lumerical and Mechanical to analyze optical behavior and material stresses from the nanometer to the macro scale.

6Proposals

Alongside the modeling, I independently proposed material responses for pressure-tunable optical behavior, drawing on how material structure responds to pressure, temperature and electromagnetic fields.

One of those proposals, a pressure-induced SHG response, was later reported by other researchers. I was not an author of that work and did not validate it experimentally.

The research gave me working exposure to transition metal dichalcogenides, 2D van der Waals materials, and the characterization and synthesis methods used to study them.
