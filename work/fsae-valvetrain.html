Valvetrain redesign for a restricted Yamaha R6

Formula SAE forces every cylinder of a 16,000 rpm engine to breathe through one 20 mm hole. Over ten weeks I measured the stock valvetrain, rebuilt it as a MATLAB model, and redesigned the cam and valve seat around the air the restrictor actually allows. I also designed the restrictor itself, for a high discharge coefficient that still fits the car.

01 — Baseline analysis

Teardown before theory

The stock cam’s true geometry wasn’t published, so I measured it. The engine came apart for cam measurement and clearancing. I measured the lobe point by point, rebuilt it digitally, and weighed every valvetrain component so the model would run on measured inputs instead of guesses.

Cleaning up a measured profile

Measured points carry noise, and differentiating lift into velocity, acceleration and jerk amplifies it. A 12-harmonic Fourier fit removed the noise. A 4.6 mm nose radius from the measured spline geometry capped the profile, and a comparison against a 3-4-5 polynomial quantified the remaining error before the profile became the baseline.

Measured inputs

Measured Component masses (spring: 30 g) were used to validate the MATLAB model.

A digital twin of the stock valvetrain

The MATLAB model reproduces stock cam timing, lift and valve motion and feeds those lift profiles into Ansys for transient CFD with dynamic meshing. The intake air path was modeled to about 2%, and the combustion chamber to about 1.2% error from limited OEM information. The same model gave the stock engine’s dynamic picture: net force on the lobe, spring surge, and friction losses.

02 — Identifying the constraint

One 20 mm hole

The R6 is built to make power at very high engine speed with unrestricted air. Above a certain speed, flow through the restrictor chokes: the throat reaches sonic velocity and no amount of valve lift draws more air. Lift and duration the restrictor can’t feed only add spring load, friction and valve-control risk. So the real question was which valvetrain matches the air this engine can actually get.

A tool for comparing cams

I wrote a MATLAB app to compare candidate cam profiles against stock on mass flow. It uses isentropic flow through a converging nozzle (NASA) and the cylinder-filling methods in Gordon P. Blair’s Design and Simulation of Four-Stroke Engines.

In the screenshot: max lift 6.5 mm, 284° duration at 1 mm, base radius 12.5 mm. Delivered flow is 55.6 g/s, 0.98× stock, against the 68.6 g/s ceiling.

03 — Restrictor design

Getting the most air through the hole

The rules fix the 20 mm throat, not the shape around it. I was in charge of designing the restrictor for a high discharge coefficient, Cd. The closer it is to 1, the less flow is lost on the way through, and the choked-flow ceiling in Fig. 02.1 scales directly with it. The highest-Cd design didn’t have the right connections, and the second didn’t fit on the car. The third is the shortest, and the one that fits.

Both runs treated air as incompressible. When I added compressibility for design 3, its Cd rose from an estimated 0.89 to 0.925, so the first two would likely read higher as well. Modeled · CFD

Built around parts that already existed

The first design’s dimensions didn’t fit the team’s existing throttle body and plenum. So I got the CAD models of both and built design 2 around them, with a flange that bolts to the throttle body. On the full car it fit every component and stayed under the main hoop, as the rules require. But the 12.1 in stack of filter, throttle body and restrictor clipped the rear wing.

Shorter, tighter and compressible

Clearing the wing meant a much shorter restrictor, while the plenum designer also wanted to extend the plenum up. I convinced him to narrow the plenum opening from 36 mm to 25 mm, so the flow would stay attached in the diverging section and the package would slim down. I also realised the earlier runs had ignored compressibility. Near Mach 0.66 that matters, so I reran the final design compressible at 20 °C. That raised its Cd from an estimated 0.89 to 0.925. The result is a restrictor only 4 in long, with a Cd of 0.925, that fits the car.

04 — Redesign

Less lift, the right motion law, softer springs

The redesign brought maximum lift down to 6.5 mm to suit restricted airflow and low-to-midrange torque, with softer springs. The motion law sets how the valve gets there. It trades time near full lift and flank steepness against peak acceleration and jerk, which drive spring load and valve control.

Two candidate lobes at 6.5 mm

A harmonic 6.5 mm lobe, and a 3-4-5 polynomial 6.5 mm lobe that holds the valve longer near maximum lift with steeper flanks. Both went forward to FEA.

A 30° back-cut ahead of the 45° seat

In Ansys I modeled the intake ports, valves and chamber, then varied the back-cut on the valve head. A 30° back-cut ahead of the 45° seat chamfer smooths the turn the air makes into the cylinder. CFD showed about 15% more airflow under the modeled conditions.

I also looked at intake timing and exhaust-gas reversion. With low plenum pressure behind a restrictor, reversion becomes more likely.

05 — Computational validation

Checked in FEA before any metal was cut

Each profile was meshed and loaded in FEA against the material’s 710 MPa yield strength. The harmonic lobe raised the safety factor above stock. The 3-4-5 lobe’s breathing advantage came with a narrow shoulder, so I refined the mesh at that point of highest Hertz contact stress.

Reading the 1.03 honestly. On paper the polynomial lobe breathes better, but its smallest-radius shoulder is the limiting feature, and FEA found it before manufacture. That version needs a larger minimum radius, or a material or surface change, before it can be cut. The 1.29 and 1.55 figures come from full-profile analyses. The 1.03 is a local contact check, so they are not directly comparable. Modeled · FEA

Friction, before and after

Smaller lift and softer springs cut modeled intake valvetrain friction from 6.0 hp to 4.1 hp at 16,000 rpm, about 2 hp. The cam also matches the air coming in, which helps drivability.

07 — Powertrain Team Work

The rest of Powertrain

Although I focused in on airflow, being a member of the powertrain team inherently means understanding and working on the whole engine. A borescope inspection found a crack in the block, which prompted a full engine teardown. I examined uneven scoring on a piston for signs of the engine running hot at a previous competition. I also contributed to the teardown and eventual rebuild of the engine. Separately, I used CFD on combustion and exhaust flow, contributed to header and plenum decisions, and helped MIG weld, manufacture and assemble the header and gearbox systems.
